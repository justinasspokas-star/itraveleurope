Travel insurance for Europe: what coverage do you need?

A useful Europe policy should match the risks in your actual trip: emergency medical care, evacuation, cancellation, delays, prepaid bookings and activities. Your country of residence still matters because it determines which policies you can buy and what health protection you may already have.

Quick answer

What should travel insurance for Europe actually cover?

For most leisure trips, the useful comparison is not “do I have insurance?” but whether the policy matches the money and medical risk in your itinerary. Look at emergency medical treatment, medical evacuation, cancellation, interruption, delays, baggage limits, pre-existing-condition wording and the activities you plan to do.

Emergency medicalCheck the maximum benefit, deductible, exclusions and whether the insurer can arrange direct payment or expects reimbursement.
Medical evacuationRead who authorizes transport, where you may be taken and whether repatriation home is included or treated separately.
CancellationInsure eligible prepaid, non-refundable costs and read the exact covered reasons.
Trip interruptionUseful when a covered event forces you to change the trip after departure and creates new transport or accommodation costs.
Delay / missed connectionCheck waiting periods, transport definitions and whether separately booked rail, ferry or low-cost airline segments qualify.
Baggage and valuablesLook at per-item limits for phones, cameras and electronics, not only the headline baggage total.

Is travel insurance required for Europe?

There is no single Europe-wide rule that makes travel insurance mandatory for every visitor. Entry requirements depend on nationality, residence status and the type of visa being used. A U.S. or Canadian tourist who is visa-exempt for a short Schengen visit is in a different position from an applicant who needs a Schengen visa. Visa applicants should use the separate Schengen visa insurance requirements guide.

Europe, the EU and the Schengen Area are not the same thing

Policy wording often uses broad geographic labels such as “Europe,” while immigration rules distinguish between the European Union, the Schengen Area and countries outside both frameworks. The United Kingdom is outside Schengen; Switzerland is in Schengen but not the EU. For a multi-country trip, do not infer coverage from the label alone—check the policy territory and declare destinations when the insurer asks for them.

Emergency medical coverage: read past the headline limit

A large medical maximum can look reassuring, but it is only one part of the comparison. Check the deductible, whether treatment must be pre-authorized, how outpatient care is handled and what happens if the hospital requires payment before discharge. Travelers with U.S. Medicare, Canadian provincial health coverage or a UK GHIC/EHIC should first understand what those arrangements do—and do not—cover abroad.

Medical evacuation deserves its own review. It may cover transport to an appropriate facility rather than a flight back home, and the assistance company may need to approve the move. That distinction matters on islands, cruises, ski trips and rural itineraries.

Trip cancellation and interruption: insure the money actually at risk

Cancellation benefits are usually tied to covered reasons and eligible trip cost. Add up the prepaid, non-refundable amounts you would genuinely lose: flights, hotels, cruises, tours, rail passes or other deposits. Refundable bookings, vouchers and credits may change the amount of a claim.

Trip interruption is different. It applies after departure when a covered event forces you to cut the trip short or rearrange it. For a complex Europe itinerary, the value may come from replacement transport and unused prepaid arrangements rather than from one large booking.

Multi-country Europe trips need one coherent coverage period

A route such as New York → Madrid → Paris → Rome → London can involve Schengen countries, a non-Schengen country, airlines, trains and separately booked hotels. One policy can often cover the full trip, but you should verify the territory, dates and transport wording. See our multi-country Europe travel insurance guide for a deeper checklist.

Single-trip vs annual multi-trip cover

A single-trip policy is built around one defined journey. Annual multi-trip insurance can make sense for frequent travelers, but compare the maximum length allowed for each individual trip, age limits, destination territory and whether higher-risk activities need an add-on. “Annual” does not automatically mean every trip of any duration is covered.

Pre-existing medical conditions and older travelers

Do not rely on labels such as “senior plan” or “pre-existing conditions covered.” Read the definition, stability or look-back period, any waiver deadline and whether a recent medication change, investigation or treatment affects eligibility. Travelers 65+ can use our dedicated Europe travel insurance over 65 guide, and U.S. travelers should also check our Medicare in Europe guide.

Activities can change the policy you need

Skiing in France, hiking in Madeira, riding a scooter on a Greek island, surfing in Portugal and joining an organized sporting event may be treated differently. Check activity definitions, altitude restrictions, off-piste rules, licensing requirements and whether rescue costs sit under medical, evacuation or a separate benefit.

A practical Europe travel insurance checklist

  1. Confirm the insurer can sell the policy to residents of your country.
  2. List every destination and the full travel dates when required.
  3. Calculate non-refundable trip cost rather than total holiday spending.
  4. Compare medical and evacuation wording separately.
  5. Check pre-existing-condition definitions and purchase deadlines.
  6. Review delay and missed-connection rules for trains and ferries.
  7. Check baggage per-item limits for expensive electronics.
  8. Read activity exclusions for skiing, hiking, scooters, diving or surfing.
  9. Save policy documents, emergency numbers and booking receipts offline.

What to compare

Medical protection is only one part of the policy.

Match the wording to your route, deposits, age, health and activities.

  1. Emergency medical: limit, deductible and whether payment is direct or reimbursed.
  2. Evacuation: who authorizes transport and where you may be taken.
  3. Cancellation: eligible non-refundable cost and covered reasons.
  4. Delay and missed connection: waiting periods for flights, rail and ferries.
  5. Pre-existing conditions and activities: exclusions, waivers and purchase deadlines.

Visa-free entry does not mean “no travel risk”.

Travel insurance is generally not a standard entry requirement for ordinary short visa-free tourism by U.S. or Canadian citizens in the Schengen Area. Medical bills, evacuation, interruption and unused bookings can still create significant costs. If you personally need a Schengen visa because of nationality or immigration status, use the visa-insurance route instead.
