Medical travel insurance

Travel insurance for pre-existing medical conditions: what to check before Europe

If you already have a medical condition, the cheapest policy is not automatically the best-value policy. Learn what insurers may ask, what to declare and which medical, evacuation and cancellation terms deserve a closer look before you travel.

By iTravelEurope Editorial TeamPublished October 5, 2026Approx. 13 min read

Quick answer

A pre-existing condition does not automatically mean you cannot buy travel insurance. But policies define medical conditions differently. The key is to answer medical screening questions accurately, confirm whether your condition and related complications are covered, and check cancellation and medical evacuation separately. Do not assume that a stable or controlled condition is irrelevant.

What is a pre-existing medical condition in travel insurance?

There is no single definition that applies to every travel insurance policy. An insurer may ask about diagnoses, medication, treatment, surgery, hospital admissions, recurring symptoms, specialist referrals, pending tests or conditions that are currently under investigation.

That matters because a condition can feel routine in everyday life while still being relevant to insurance. High blood pressure controlled by medication, asthma that rarely causes symptoms or a previous serious illness may still fall within an insurer's questions or policy definition.

Common information an insurer may ask about

Diagnosis, current medication, recent treatment, hospital stays, specialist consultations, planned procedures and whether symptoms have changed recently.

Do not self-filter your medical history

If the insurer asks about it, answer it. Deciding that a condition is “not serious enough” to mention can create problems later if a claim is connected to that condition.

UK government travel guidance specifically advises travelers to declare existing physical or mental health conditions, including conditions under investigation and pending treatment or tests.

Does travel insurance cover pre-existing conditions?

Sometimes. The outcome depends on the insurer, policy, medical history and answers given during medical screening. A condition may be accepted within the policy, accepted with different terms or price, excluded from related claims, or require a specialist provider.

Condition accepted

The insurer may include eligible emergencies related to the declared condition, subject to limits and exclusions.

Additional premium

Some insurers may offer cover but price the policy differently after medical screening.

Condition excluded

You may still have insurance for other risks while claims connected with the excluded condition are not covered.

Specialist policy

Travelers with more complex histories may need to compare providers that specifically handle medical-condition cover.

MoneyHelper notes that insurers use different definitions and that conditions awaiting an operation, pending test results or serious previous illnesses can be relevant. It also points travelers toward specialist providers when mainstream cover is difficult to obtain.

Why medical disclosure matters before you travel

Imagine you have a known heart condition that has been stable for years. While traveling in Italy, you develop chest pain and need emergency hospital care. At claim stage, the question may not simply be whether you bought travel insurance. The insurer may also check whether the relevant medical history was disclosed as required and whether the condition was covered.

This is why the useful question is not “Can I avoid mentioning this?” but “How does this policy treat my actual medical situation?” Medical disclosure is part of understanding the product you are buying.

The U.S. National Association of Insurance Commissioners also advises consumers to check whether pre-existing conditions are excluded and whether insurer approval is required before certain medical care.

Controlled does not necessarily mean irrelevant

A common mistake is assuming that a condition no longer matters because it is well controlled. Diabetes, asthma, high blood pressure, high cholesterol, heart conditions, respiratory disease, previous cancer, epilepsy and mental health conditions can all be relevant depending on the insurer's questions.

The right approach is simple: answer the medical questions accurately and let the insurer determine whether the condition changes eligibility, price or coverage.

Do not think only about hospital bills

Pre-existing medical conditions can affect several different parts of a travel insurance policy. Looking only at the emergency medical limit can leave important gaps.

Emergency medical treatment

Check the medical limit, deductible, private-versus-public treatment rules and whether the insurer expects you to contact assistance before non-routine care.

Medical evacuation

Treatment and transport are separate benefits. Check who authorizes evacuation, where you may be taken and the maximum benefit.

Medical repatriation

If you cannot take your original flight home, medically necessary transport or an escort can create significant costs. Confirm how the policy handles repatriation.

Trip interruption

If illness ends a trip early, look at unused bookings and the extra transport costs that may be eligible under the policy.

Pre-existing conditions can affect cancellation cover too

This is one of the most important points to check. Suppose you have €4,000 of non-refundable flights, hotels, tours and cruise deposits. Two weeks before departure, your existing medical condition worsens and your doctor advises you not to travel.

The relevant question becomes: does the cancellation section cover a cancellation connected to that pre-existing condition? A policy can have a high overseas medical limit while applying different conditions to cancellation. Compare both sections instead of treating “medical cover” as one single benefit.

What if you are waiting for tests or do not have a diagnosis yet?

Do not assume “no diagnosis” means “nothing to declare.” Recurring symptoms, abnormal test results, a specialist referral, a pending MRI, planned surgery or a condition currently under investigation can be relevant to medical screening.

GOV.UK specifically tells travelers to declare pending treatment or tests so that related complications can be considered. The exact requirement for you depends on the insurer's wording and questions.

Mental health conditions can be relevant too

Medical screening is not necessarily limited to physical conditions. Depending on the insurer and policy, diagnosed mental health conditions may also need to be disclosed. GOV.UK advises travelers to declare mental health conditions and notes that some policies may exclude treatment related to a pre-existing mental health condition.

Again, do not guess what the insurer considers relevant. Answer the questions you are asked and check whether treatment, cancellation or interruption connected to the condition is covered.

10 things to check before buying

  1. How the policy defines a pre-existing medical condition.
  2. Whether your specific condition is accepted after medical screening.
  3. Whether complications related to that condition are covered or excluded.
  4. Whether pending tests, referrals or planned treatment affect eligibility.
  5. The emergency medical limit and deductible.
  6. The medical evacuation and repatriation benefit and authorization rules.
  7. Whether cancellation related to your condition can be covered.
  8. Whether trip interruption and additional transport are included.
  9. What to do if your health, diagnosis or medication changes after purchase.
  10. The 24/7 assistance number and process for arranging treatment abroad.

Example: diabetes and a trip to Spain

A traveler with controlled type 2 diabetes should not compare only the cheapest quote for Spain. A more useful comparison asks whether diabetes is accepted, whether related complications are covered, how medical treatment and evacuation work, what deductible applies, and whether a deterioration before departure could affect cancellation cover.

For Spain specifically, UK government guidance recommends having appropriate travel insurance for local treatment or unexpected medical evacuation and notes that GHIC or EHIC is not a replacement for travel insurance.

Example: a previous heart condition on a multi-country Europe trip

For a London → Paris → Switzerland → Italy itinerary, the traveler should check whether the condition is accepted, whether the policy covers every destination and the full trip duration, how emergency transportation is handled, and whether a medically necessary early return could be covered.

GHIC or EHIC does not replace this check

Eligible UK travelers may have access to medically necessary state healthcare through a valid GHIC or eligible EHIC in participating countries. That is different from a travel insurance contract and does not replace benefits such as cancellation or medical repatriation.

A better way to compare policies

When a medical condition is involved, compare in this order:

  1. Can the insurer sell this policy to a resident of my country?
  2. Is my medical condition accepted?
  3. Are related complications covered?
  4. Are the medical and evacuation limits suitable for my trip?
  5. Does cancellation match my prepaid non-refundable costs?
  6. What exclusions, deductibles and authorization rules apply?
  7. Only then compare the premium.

Ready to compare? Start with your residence, destination and trip dates, then read the medical-condition wording before you buy.

What information is useful to have ready?

  • Your exact diagnosis, if known.
  • Current prescription medication.
  • Recent treatment or hospital admissions.
  • Previous surgery relevant to the condition.
  • Specialist appointments, pending tests or planned treatment.
  • Your destination countries and full travel dates.
  • Your total prepaid, non-refundable trip cost.

The insurer may not ask for all of this, but accurate information makes medical screening easier and reduces the temptation to answer from memory.

The most important rule

Do not hide a condition just to obtain a cheaper quote.

If the insurer asks about something, answer accurately. Travel insurance becomes most important when something goes seriously wrong, which is exactly when unclear or incomplete medical disclosure can become consequential.

A cheaper policy can still be the right policy. But price should come after eligibility and coverage, not before them.

Frequently asked questions

Does travel insurance automatically cover pre-existing medical conditions?

No. Coverage varies by insurer and policy. A condition may be covered, excluded, accepted after medical screening or subject to specific eligibility requirements. Read the policy definition and answer the insurer's medical questions accurately.

What counts as a pre-existing medical condition for travel insurance?

Definitions vary. Diagnosed conditions, medication, recent treatment, doctor consultations, pending tests, referrals or serious previous illnesses may be relevant depending on the policy and screening questions.

Do I need to declare a condition if it is controlled?

If the insurer's questions require it, yes. A stable or controlled condition can still be relevant to underwriting and claims, so do not decide for the insurer that a condition is too minor to mention.

Can a pending test or undiagnosed symptom matter?

Yes. Some policies and medical screening questions treat conditions under investigation, pending test results or planned treatment as relevant. Check the exact wording before buying.

Can pre-existing conditions affect trip cancellation cover?

Yes. A condition can affect not only emergency medical treatment abroad but also cancellation or interruption if illness prevents travel or ends a trip early. Check both sections separately.

Should I tell the insurer if my health changes after buying the policy?

Check the policy and contact the insurer. Requirements vary, so a new diagnosis, medication change, hospital admission or planned treatment may need to be reported before departure.
