Pregnancy · medical travel insurance

Travel insurance for pregnancy in Europe: what to check before you go

Pregnancy does not automatically make travel insurance unnecessary — or automatically covered. The important questions are what pregnancy-related emergencies are included, whether the policy has a gestational-week limit, and what happens with premature birth, newborn care, evacuation and cancellation.

By iTravelEurope Editorial TeamReviewed October 12, 2026Approx. 15 min read

Quick answer

Travel insurance can cover unexpected pregnancy-related complications, but it does not mean every pregnancy expense is covered. Routine prenatal care, planned scans, normal childbirth, treatment that was already expected and care for a newborn may be excluded or limited. The policy's gestational-week rules and medical definitions matter more than the headline medical limit.

Does travel insurance cover pregnancy in Europe?

Often, pregnancy itself is not treated as an illness. But travel insurance is designed mainly for unexpected events, so the policy may distinguish between a normal pregnancy and an unforeseen complication that requires urgent medical care.

May be covered

An unexpected pregnancy complication requiring emergency assessment, hospital treatment or medically necessary care, if it falls within the policy's pregnancy terms and gestational limits.

Do not assume it is covered

Routine prenatal appointments, planned scans, normal maternity care, a planned birth abroad, or treatment you already knew would be needed.

Needs a separate question

Premature birth, caesarean section, neonatal intensive care, treatment of the newborn and medical evacuation.

The most important question: up to what week are you covered?

There is no universal pregnancy week limit for travel insurance. One insurer may use one gestational limit for pregnancy complications, another may use a different limit, and cancellation cover can have separate rules again.

Do not search for one magic number such as “travel insurance pregnant 28 weeks” and assume it applies to every policy. Instead, calculate how many weeks pregnant you will be on each day of the trip and ask the insurer how its wording applies.

Premature birth and newborn care: the gap travelers often miss

A policy can advertise a high emergency medical limit and still have restrictions around childbirth or the newborn. That is especially important because neonatal care can involve a long hospital stay and specialist treatment.

The CDC specifically advises pregnant travelers to check whether their health or travel insurance covers pregnancy complications and neonatal care, and to consider medical evacuation insurance where appropriate.

Ask this exact question before buying

“If I unexpectedly go into premature labour during this trip, what costs are covered for me, what costs are covered for the baby, and what gestational-week limit applies?”

EHIC and pregnancy: useful, but not a replacement for travel insurance

For travelers who are entitled to use a European Health Insurance Card, the EHIC can cover medically necessary state-provided healthcare during a temporary stay in participating European countries. Official EU guidance says this includes necessary pregnancy care and unplanned childbirth.

But the EHIC does not cover every travel risk. It does not cover private healthcare, repatriation home or ordinary travel losses such as cancellation and baggage. It also is not intended for a trip whose purpose is planned medical treatment or planned childbirth abroad.

U.S. and Canadian travelers: do not assume domestic health insurance covers maternity care abroad

If you are travelling from the United States or Canada, check your domestic health plan before departure. Overseas coverage varies, and direct billing is not guaranteed.

The CDC warns that many health insurance policies do not cover pregnancy or neonatal complications outside the United States. For a Europe trip, compare travel medical insurance not only for the mother, but also for pregnancy-related emergencies, newborn care and evacuation.

What if you already have a pregnancy complication?

A complication that has already been diagnosed can be treated differently from a new, unexpected event. Depending on the policy, it may fall under a pre-existing medical condition, be excluded, require medical screening or be covered only in limited circumstances.

Examples could include a history of threatened preterm labour, placenta-related complications, hypertension in pregnancy or another condition requiring closer monitoring. The exact insurance treatment depends on the wording and your medical history.

Can travel insurance cover trip cancellation because of pregnancy?

Normal pregnancy or simply deciding not to travel is not automatically a covered cancellation reason. But some policies can cover unexpected pregnancy complications when they meet the policy's definition of a covered event and medically prevent travel.

Cancellation rules are separate from medical treatment abroad. Check the pregnancy wording in both sections of the policy.

Flying while pregnant: airline rules are separate from insurance rules

A travel insurer can consider you covered while an airline still refuses boarding under its own pregnancy policy. The two sets of rules are separate.

The NHS advises that many people can fly during an uncomplicated pregnancy, but airlines may ask for a medical letter after around week 28 and can apply their own limits later in pregnancy. The chance of labour is naturally higher after 37 weeks, or around 32 weeks with twins, so airline restrictions can become more relevant near term.

Always check the operating airline's current pregnancy policy before departure and before buying a non-refundable ticket.

Long flights to Europe: what to plan for

Long-distance travel can add practical concerns such as prolonged sitting, dehydration and access to medical care. Your healthcare professional can advise on your individual risk, especially if you have a high-risk pregnancy or a history of complications.

For insurance purposes, the most useful preparation is to know where you would seek care, carry your policy and assistance details, and make sure the insurer can arrange or authorise treatment if required.

What documents should you carry?

  1. Travel insurance certificate and policy number.
  2. 24-hour assistance contact details.
  3. EHIC/GHIC if you are entitled to use one.
  4. Pregnancy summary with estimated due date and relevant medical history.
  5. Current medication list using generic drug names where possible.
  6. Airline fit-to-fly letter if required for your gestational stage.
  7. Contact details for your maternity provider at home.
  8. Details of hospitals with obstetric and neonatal care at your destination if you are later in pregnancy or higher risk.

What to ask an insurer before buying pregnancy travel insurance

  1. Is pregnancy itself treated as a medical condition under this policy?
  2. What unexpected pregnancy complications are covered?
  3. What gestational-week limit applies on my travel dates?
  4. Does the limit apply at departure, return or the date treatment is needed?
  5. Is premature labour covered?
  6. Is emergency caesarean section covered?
  7. Is newborn or neonatal care covered separately?
  8. Are there sublimits for pregnancy-related claims?
  9. Can pregnancy complications trigger medical evacuation?
  10. Are pregnancy complications a covered cancellation reason?
  11. How are pre-existing pregnancy complications treated?
  12. Do I need approval before attending a private hospital?

Best practice

If pregnancy cover is important to your trip, ask the insurer to confirm the answer in writing. A generic statement that the policy has “medical cover” is not enough.

What to do if you need urgent pregnancy care in Europe

  1. In an emergency in the EU, call 112.
  2. Contact the insurer's emergency assistance line as soon as reasonably possible.
  3. Use the public system if relying on EHIC, because private treatment is not covered by EHIC.
  4. Keep medical reports, invoices and receipts.
  5. Ask before arranging expensive private transport or evacuation unless delay would be unsafe.
  6. Tell the insurer immediately if hospitalisation or premature delivery is possible so newborn-cover questions can be addressed early.

Frequently asked questions

Does travel insurance cover pregnancy in Europe?

It may cover unexpected pregnancy-related complications, but routine prenatal care, planned treatment, childbirth and newborn care can have separate limits or exclusions. Check the exact policy wording.

Up to what week of pregnancy does travel insurance cover me?

There is no universal week limit. Insurers can use different gestational limits for complications, childbirth and cancellation, so calculate your week during the trip and confirm the rule with the insurer.

Does EHIC cover pregnancy care in Europe?

For eligible EHIC holders, necessary state-provided pregnancy care during a temporary stay can be covered, including unplanned childbirth. EHIC does not cover private healthcare, repatriation or normal travel-insurance risks.

Does travel insurance cover premature birth?

Do not assume it does. Check pregnancy-complication wording, gestational limits, childbirth exclusions and any specific sublimit for premature delivery.

Does travel insurance cover a newborn baby after an emergency birth?

Not automatically. Neonatal care can be extremely important and may be subject to separate limits or exclusions. Ask specifically about the newborn before buying.

Can I cancel a trip because of pregnancy complications?

Some policies can cover unexpected complications if they meet the policy's covered-reason definition and medically prevent travel. Normal pregnancy or changing your mind is not automatically covered.

Can an airline stop me flying even if my insurance still covers me?

Yes. Airline pregnancy rules and insurance coverage are separate. Check both before the trip.
